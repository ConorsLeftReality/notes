Translating into Logic

Specifying how complex systems behave (who can access what, what a module should do, how two programs talk) is a key skill. If we get the spec into logic, we can analyse it and check an implementation meets it

E.G. specs: "You can read this page if you are a CS student", "If the email is from a blacklisted address or contains the word viagra and the sender is not in the address book, it goes to Spam"

English to p → q

All of these mean p → q:

  • if p then q
  • p implies q
  • p only if q
  • p is sufficient for q
  • q follows from p
  • q whenever p
  • q when p
  • q is necessary for p

Watch out: in the last few, p and q have switched places in the sentence

BUT BEWARE

Still check that it's what was actually meant. And don't translate back from logic into English using words like "implies" or "is sufficient for", it's easy to get wrong

Problems with English

  • Unclear precedence and grouping (which "and"/"or" goes with what)
  • Inclusive vs exclusive OR - in logic, OR means "one or both"
  • Conditional sentences - "if you don't eat your main course, you can't have any pudding". Does the logic match what the speaker meant, or what the child expected?

E.G. "You can read this page if you are a CS student" is q → p (q = CS student, p = can read). By the truth table, a non-CS student reading the page is still allowed (F → T is T). Is that what we meant?

Contrapositive, Converse and Inverse

For p → q:

  • Contrapositive: ¬q → ¬p - EQUIVALENT
  • Converse: q → p - NOT equivalent
  • Inverse: ¬p → ¬q - NOT equivalent

p → q is also equivalent to ¬p ∨ q

(Check them with truth tables, the contrapositive column matches p → q, the other two don't)

E.G. "If I am in Cork, then I am in Ireland":

  • "If I am in Ireland, then I am in Cork" - converse, different
  • "Either I am not in Cork or I am in Ireland" - equivalent
  • "If I am not in Cork, then I am not in Ireland" - inverse, different
  • "If I am not in Ireland, then I am not in Cork" - contrapositive, equivalent

Logical Fallacies

A logical fallacy is a bad argument: the conclusion isn't guaranteed to be true when the premises are. They're everywhere (politicians, newspapers, conversations), so learn to spot them

  • Affirming the consequent: p → q, q, therefore p - NOT VALID. "If I'm at work, I'm wearing a tie. I'm wearing a tie. So I'm at work"
  • Denying the hypothesis: p → q, ¬p, therefore ¬q - NOT VALID. "If I click dodgy links, I'll get a virus. I don't click dodgy links. So I won't get a virus"
  • Affirming the disjunct: p ∨ q, p, therefore ¬q - NOT VALID. "It'll rain or the sun will shine. It rained. So the sun didn't shine". Only works if the OR is exclusive, and in logic OR is inclusive

Another way to check an argument

Make one statement: (premises joined with ∧) → conclusion. If it's a tautology, the argument is valid

E.G. modus ponens: (p ∧ (p → q)) → q is T in every row, so it's valid
