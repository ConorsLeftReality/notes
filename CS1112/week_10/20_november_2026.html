Logical Arguments - Lecture 15

NOTE

The Lecture 15 slides wouldn't load when this was written, so this section is the standard material - check it against the slides

An argument is some premises and a conclusion. It's valid if whenever all the premises are true, the conclusion is true too

Check it with a truth table: look at every row where all the premises are T - the conclusion must be T in all of them

  • Modus ponens: p ⇒ q, p, therefore q
  • Modus tollens: p ⇒ q, ¬q, therefore ¬p

Bad Arguments and Translation - Lecture 16

Logic is how we write specifications precisely. English is messy, so be careful translating

These all mean p ⇒ q: "if p then q", "p implies q", "p only if q", "p is sufficient for q", "q is necessary for p", "q whenever p", "q follows from p"

Related statements for p ⇒ q

  • Contrapositive ¬q ⇒ ¬p - equivalent to the original
  • Converse q ⇒ p - NOT equivalent
  • Inverse ¬p ⇒ ¬q - NOT equivalent

Classic fallacies: assuming the converse (affirming the consequent) or the inverse (denying the antecedent). E.g. "if you don't eat your dinner you can't have pudding" doesn't actually promise pudding if you do
