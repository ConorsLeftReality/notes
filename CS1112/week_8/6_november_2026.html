Intro To Logic

Logic is the study of rational argument

Given a set of facts, and an argument claiming that a conclusion follows from those facts, how do we decide whether or not the argument is justified?

In order to do this, we need a precise language to state the facts, the conclusions and the steps of the argument.

E.G. "If Ireland are playing in Dublin, they wear green. Ireland are wearing green. Therefore Ireland are playing in Dublin" is not a valid argument, even though it sounds reasonable.

History of Logic

Studied since the greeks, Aristotle (c350BC) analysed argument, and his methods were used for more or less 2000 years.

George Boole, here at UCC, published "An Investigation of the Laws of Thought" in 1854

Boom, boolean logic. Boole's work made modern digital computers possible, and led to symbolic logic (started by Gottlob Frege in 1879)

Logic in Computer Science

Logic is about cold, calm, rational argument (not emotions or who shouts loudest). In CS we use it to design circuits, decide which branch of an if runs, specify what programs must do, test and prove programs correct, check security systems and databases, and build AI/expert systems.

Humans are generally bad at logic (we jump to conclusions), and in CS logical mistakes can be serious - e.g. bank security code, pharmaceutical processes, power station emergency sequences.

Logical Testing

E.G. a method must return false if its input is even. To test it, what do you actually need to check? Random even inputs (must give false) are needed. Random odd inputs tell you nothing, because the spec doesn't say what odd inputs should give. Getting the spec's logic right tells you what's worth testing.

What does CS1112 look at?

CS1112 looks at propositional logic: simple statements that are either true or false, how to write them, how to build complex statements, how to work out if they're true or false, and how to prove them. (CS1113 moves on to predicate logic, with variables and collections.)

Propositions

An atomic statement, or proposition, is a phrase or sentence that declares a fact, and is either true or false. E.G. "Dublin is the capital of Ireland" (true), "Homer Simpson lives in Cork" (false), "7 + 2 = 9" (true).

Not propositions: questions ("Are we nearly there yet?"), commands ("Yield at the junction."), exclamations ("Ouch!"), and anything with unbound variables like "x = y+2" (could be true or false depending on x and y).

True (T) and False (F) are the truth values. Each proposition has exactly one, so there's a truth function from propositions to {T, F}.

Logical Compound Statements

We build complex statements by combining simple ones with connectives. The truth value of the whole thing depends on the truth values of the parts and which connective is used. We write propositions as p, q, r, ...

Logic Symbols

¬ - Not, ∧ - And, ∨ - Or, ⇒ - Implies (conditional), ⇔ - Biconditional

Negation (¬)

Applies to a single proposition and just flips its truth value. If p = "Dublin is the capital of Ireland" (T), then ¬p = "Dublin is not the capital of Ireland" (F).

Disjunction (∨) - Inclusive OR

p ∨ q is true if one or both of p and q are true, and false only if both are false.

The English word "or" is ambiguous: "asparagus or paté" on a menu probably means one but not both (exclusive or). In logic, ∨ is always the inclusive or.

Conditional Statements (⇒)

p ⇒ q ("if p then q") is false only when p is true and q is false. Whenever p is false, the conditional is true no matter what q is.

P Q P ⇒ Q
T T T
T F F
F T T
F F T
Beware!

⇒ does not mean "causes". It only says that whenever p is true, q is also true. E.G. "the moon is made of blue cheese ⇒ anything" is true, because p is false.

The conditional as a contract: p = "you get 40% in CS1112", q = "you get a pass". UCC promises p ⇒ q. If you get 40% and pass, the contract is kept (T). If you get 40% and don't pass, it's broken (F). If you get under 40%, UCC hasn't promised anything, so whether you pass or not, the contract isn't broken (T, T).

Biconditional (⇔)

p ⇔ q ("p if and only if q") is true whenever p and q have the same truth value, and false otherwise.

TTT
TFF
FTF
FFT

E.G. "I am qualified to enter 2nd year ⇔ I gain 50 credits in 1st year". Same warnings as the conditional: ⇔ doesn't mean "is the same thing as", just that the two have the same truth value.
